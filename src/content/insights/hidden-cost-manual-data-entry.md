In most food plants, the same data gets entered three, four, sometimes five times. A weight from the scale gets written on a ticket, then entered into a spreadsheet, then keyed into the ERP, then copied to a quality report. Every re-entry is a cost — and not just in labor.

## The Obvious Cost: Labor Hours

Start with the math. If a receiving clerk spends 15 minutes per load entering data that a scale could capture automatically, and you receive 40 loads a day, that's 10 hours of data entry — every day. Multiply by the number of data entry touch points across your operation (receiving, production, quality, warehouse, shipping) and the hours add up fast.

But labor is actually the *smallest* cost of manual data entry.

## The Hidden Costs

### Errors
Manual entry has a well-documented error rate of 1–3%. In food manufacturing, a transposition error on a lot number means broken traceability. A wrong weight means wrong inventory. A miskeyed temperature reading means a quality deviation that nobody catches until an auditor asks about it.

### Latency
When data is entered hours or days after the event, you're managing your plant with yesterday's information. You can't make real-time decisions about production yield, inventory levels, or quality trends when the data hasn't been entered yet.

### Compliance Risk
Auditors don't just want to see the data — they want to see when it was captured. A batch record that was filled in three days after production raises questions. An automatically captured timestamp at the point of action doesn't.

### Opportunity Cost
Every hour your skilled operators spend on data entry is an hour they're not spending on the work they were actually hired to do — running production, managing quality, solving problems.

## The Alternative

Modern plant technology captures data at the source: scales transmit weights directly, sensors record temperatures automatically, barcode scans capture lot numbers without keystroke. Each data point is entered once, at the moment it happens, and flows to every system that needs it.

No re-keying. No reconciliation. No three-day-old data treated as current.

## Where to Start

Pick the data entry activity that happens most often and costs the most in errors. For most plants, that's either receiving weights or production batch records. Automate that one workflow, prove the value, and expand from there.

The technology isn't the hard part. Understanding the operation well enough to build it right — that's what matters.
